Where does reinforcement learning pay off in mobile manipulation?

A semester project on the course's Pick–Nav–Reach task (CS5478, Intelligent Robots). We set out to solve the default project with reinforcement learning (RL), found that RL added nothing to it, and asked where it would: by giving the Fetch robot a 16-joint dexterous hand.

Where hand-engineering runs out. With the Fetch's parallel gripper, a classical grasp planner delivers 85% of objects through unseen mazes and RL adds nothing on top of it. With a 16-joint Allegro hand no grasp planner exists: a scripted grasp delivers 40%, imitating it 49%, and RL on top of imitation 76%. The learned hand also delivers cylinders too wide for the gripper's jaws. RL needs the imitation prior to get started, and on-policy PPO beats the demonstration-driven off-policy RLPD by a wide margin.

1The task

The course's default project runs in PyBullet. A Fetch mobile manipulator starts in a start room, drives to a table, picks up one of five household objects (foam brick, banana, soup can, apple, mug), carries it through a maze it has never seen, and holds it near a goal 0.9 m above the floor. The course supplies the robot, objects, maze generator and success test; how to solve it is ours to choose.

The classical pipeline: antipodal grasps, IK and A*explainer

The planner never learns anything. It samples pairs of points on the object's point cloud whose surface normals face each other closely enough that both contacts can hold by friction (an antipodal grasp), solves inverse kinematics (IK) for a collision-free arm pose that puts the jaws there, and moves the arm along a collision-checked path. For the carry it runs A* over the maze's occupancy grid and drives waypoint to waypoint; for the reach it servos the base and torso.

\[ \text{antipodal: } \angle\big(\mathbf n_1,\,-\mathbf n_2\big) \le \arctan\mu \quad\text{and}\quad \angle\big(\mathbf n_i,\ \mathbf p_2-\mathbf p_1\big)\ \text{within the friction cone} \]

Two parallel jaws need exactly two such contacts, so the search is small and complete. That is what makes the gripper easy to hand-engineer, and the reason this project ended up being about end effectors.

2The plan we proposed

Our proposal: learn the skills with off-policy RL, comparing SAC and TD3, both with hindsight experience replay (HER), on the original gripper and on a modified one, with three seeds each. That makes twelve trained systems, compared on learning efficiency and full-task success. A fixed supervisor would sequence the skills; the skills themselves would be learned.

SAC and TD3: off-policy actor-criticsexplainer

Both learn a critic \(Q(s,a)\) from stored transitions and push the actor towards actions the critic values. Both keep two critics and use the smaller estimate in the target, which damps the overestimation that otherwise compounds through bootstrapping. SAC trains a stochastic policy that also maximises entropy:

\[ y = r + \gamma\Big(\min_{i=1,2} Q_{\bar\theta_i}(s',a') - \alpha \log \pi(a'\mid s')\Big),\quad a'\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s') \qquad\text{(SAC)} \]

\[ y = r + \gamma \min_{i=1,2} Q_{\bar\theta_i}\big(s',\ \pi_{\bar\phi}(s') + \epsilon\big),\quad \epsilon\sim\operatorname{clip}(\mathcal N(0,\sigma),-c,c) \qquad\text{(TD3)} \]

TD3 was proposed but never run. By the time the SAC system worked, the bottleneck was clearly not the choice between two members of the same algorithm family: on the gripper, RL of either kind had little left to add (section 3). We dropped it on 2026-10-03 and spent the effort on the hand instead.

Hindsight experience replay (HER)explainer

For goal-conditioned tasks HER stores each episode a second time with the goal replaced by what was actually achieved, \(r(s,a,g') \) recomputed. It turns failures into examples of reaching somewhere. Its limit mattered for us: it only relabels outcomes that happened. If the object never moves, every relabelled goal is still "the object stays on the table", and nothing is learned about grasping.

3Making the full task work (PyBullet)

We built the task end to end with a fixed supervisor and three learned skills. Planned motions bring the arm to a pregrasp and into a carry posture; the grasp, the maze carry and the final reach are neural policies.

  1. 1
    Pure SAC+HER learned neither the grasp nor the carry. Random exploration almost never lifts an object, and HER cannot relabel a lift that never happened. Fix: demonstrations from the planner: behaviour cloning to initialise the actor, demonstrations in the replay buffer, and later a cloning term in SAC's actor loss to keep it near them. With that, one maze and one object passed three fresh full-task runs (milestone 1). worked

Behaviour cloning and demonstration-regularised SACexplainer

Behaviour cloning (BC) is supervised learning on a demonstrator's state–action pairs:

\[ \min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{(s,a^\ast)\sim\mathcal D}\,\big\lVert \mu_\theta(s) - a^\ast \big\rVert^2 \]

A cloned policy drifts into states the demonstrator never visited and has no data for: compounding error. Continuing with SAC can repair that, but SAC's critic is poor where data is scarce, and the actor follows its errors. We added the cloning loss to SAC's actor objective, \( \mathcal L_\pi^{\text{SAC}} + \beta\,\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\lVert\pi(s)-a^\ast\rVert^2 \) with \(\beta = 100\). It kept the policies stable, but also kept them close to the demonstrator, which is one reason RL added little.

Where this left us
RL was not doing the work. Imitating a planner was, and the planner was nearly as good on its own. To give RL a real job, the robot needed something that is hard to hand-engineer.

4A dexterous hand, and a new simulator

A 16-joint hand is hard to hand-engineer: no grasp planner exists for it, and a scripted grasp must coordinate many contacts at once. Before committing, we tested whether one would even hold objects through the carry in the course's simulator. We mounted an Allegro hand on a six-axis gantry that replays recorded carry motions, used scripted power grasps, and compared it with the Fetch gripper on the same routes.

In PyBullet the hand held worse than the gripper: 20 of 50 objects through recorded carries against 40, and 14 of 25 against 18 on unseen routes after a 5,400-trial search for robust grasp settings. Outcomes flipped with millimetre changes in placement. We stopped and worked out why. Two factors turned out to matter, and we changed both.

Factor 1: the contact simulation

We rebuilt the identical task in MuJoCo: same robot model, objects, mazes, seeds, motor law, friction coefficients and grip force, with kinematics and motor response checked against PyBullet. The gripper planner's carry drops mostly disappeared: 96 of 100 course cases instead of 76 (dev mazes: 250 of 260 instead of 190). Since everything else was matched, PyBullet's drops came from how it resolves contacts under the motion jolts of the carry. A simulator whose grasps flip on millimetres would also make any comparison of end effectors meaningless.

Why contact handling decides graspsexplainer

A held object stays put only while every contact force lies inside its friction cone, \( \lVert \mathbf f_t \rVert \le \mu f_n \). Simulators enforce this approximately. A pyramidal cone (a few linear facets) lets contacts slide slightly along the facet directions, and soft contacts that allow millimetres of penetration let the object creep a little every tick. A two-jaw grasp has two such contacts; a hand grasp has five to fifteen, each with its own small error, so the errors stack. In MuJoCo we used elliptic cones with no-slip correction, which removed most of the creep. The settings mattered as much as the engine: on the same 20 planner runs, MuJoCo's defaults succeeded 4 times (PyBullet 15); elliptic cones and no-slip correction together, 19 times.

Factor 2: the physics settings

In MuJoCo with the course's own settings the hand still failed. RL taught it to lift only the foam brick and the banana, and a frozen grasp survived 1 of 10 maze carries. The limit was now physical: the course's fingers are slippery (\(\mu = 0.25\)) and its motors are unlimited, so every base command jolts the carried object at 25–30 m/s². The course gripper survives that by squeezing with about 450 N; a hand of realistic strength squeezes with 15–40 N.

We therefore switched both end effectors to the same realistic physics: rubber grip (\(\mu = 1.0\)) and joint velocity, acceleration and effort limits from the hardware (Fetch jaws ≤ 245 N, Allegro joints ≤ 0.7 N·m). The gripper planner was unaffected (99 of 100 hold-out cases); the hand became learnable (section 6).

What we did not test
We never ran the hand in PyBullet with realistic physics. The evidence that PyBullet's contact handling was at fault comes from the gripper, whose drops vanished when only the simulator changed. "PyBullet cannot simulate a multi-finger grasp" is an inference, not a measurement.

5Same result in MuJoCo, and the real question

We retrained the whole phase-one system in MuJoCo. On 100 fresh hold-out cases: planner 98, imitation of the planner 92, imitation + SAC 92 (realistic physics: 99, 77, 77). Again RL added nothing, and in MuJoCo the planner was simply best. That reframed the project:

Where does RL add value in mobile manipulation, and how does that depend on the end effector's complexity?

Our hypothesis: RL pays off where a good hand-written controller is hard to build. For two parallel jaws an antipodal planner is close to the ceiling; for sixteen joints there is no planner and scripts are weak. To test it fairly, both end effectors must face the same task, the same learning recipe and the same evaluation. We built towards that in two attempts.

6Teaching the hand to grasp

We started on an idealised rig: the Allegro hand on a six-axis wrist over the course table, under realistic physics, with the task "grasp, lift 15 cm, then hold through a carry motion".

What we tried on the rig

PPO from scratch: the hand learned to press and scoop, never a 15 cm lift.
Curriculum from easy objects and near-grasp starts: 0% after 10M steps.
Cloning the teacher: 14%; the clone drifts off the teacher's states.
Cloning, then PPO with a frozen-actor value warm-up: 97%, above the teacher it learned from.
Cloning interleaved during PPO (DAPG-style), to stay near the teacher: held at 14–33%.

Mistake: the hand learned to hover
Our first imitation-then-PPO runs rose and then collapsed after about 4M steps, with peak lifts settling at 14.2–15.0 cm, just under the 15 cm success height. Holding a lift below the threshold paid up to ~7.5 reward per step, entering the carry phase only ~1.5, so PPO learned to hover. The fix: the carry phase adds its bonus on top of the grasp reward, so carrying always pays more. RL optimises the reward you wrote, not the one you meant.
PPO, and why the value warm-up mattersexplainer

Proximal policy optimisation (PPO) is on-policy: it collects fresh rollouts with the current policy, estimates how much better each action was than expected (the advantage \(\hat A_t\), from a learned value function), takes a few gradient steps, and discards the data. The clipped objective stops one batch from moving the policy too far:

\[ L(\theta) = \mathbb E_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t \hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\big)\Big],\qquad \rho_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)} \]

Warm-up: after cloning, the actor is good but the value function is random, so the first advantages are noise and the first updates destroy the cloned behaviour. Training the value function for 1M steps with the actor frozen gives PPO meaningful advantages from its first update.

Settings (identical for both end effectors): rollouts of 256 decisions on each of 22 workers (5,632 transitions per update), 5 epochs over minibatches of 4,096, discount \(\gamma = 0.99\), GAE \(\lambda = 0.95\), clip \(\epsilon = 0.2\), no entropy bonus, learning rate \(10^{-4}\) (chosen by the tuning sweep in section 8).

Onto the Fetch

A policy trained on the idealised rig transferred poorly to the real arm (3 of 24 lifts), so the hand was mounted on the Fetch's wrist and trained there, inside the full robot's kinematics and joint limits. In the full task the hand reuses everything planned: a planned approach brings it to the same pregrasp it was trained from, the learned policy grasps, and a hand-specific carry posture (object at goal height, arm compact and clear of the body), A* and the reach do the rest.

8The final study

Inside the grasp policy

Both end effectors use the same policy design; only the input and output sizes differ. It makes 48 decisions per second (one every five physics ticks), so the 300-decision grasp budget is about six seconds.

  • Surface points instead of an object label. Thirty-two points on the object's surface tell the policy the shape it is holding, so one policy covers all five objects without knowing which one it has.
  • Small changes, not absolute targets. Each decision nudges the wrist and the finger targets, and zero action holds still. With absolute finger targets, exploration noise reopened the grasp at every step and PPO learned nothing in 3M steps.
  • A short leash on the wrist. Clamping the wrist target near the pregrasp keeps the policy's job local: it cannot drive the arm into the table or out of reach, and cannot build up large pressing forces.
  • Last action and velocities as inputs. The joints are rate- and effort-limited, so where the hand goes next depends on recent commands; feeding them back removes that hidden state.
  • Same networks for both end effectors. Separate actor and critic of 512, 512 and 256 units, standard for state-based manipulation; a fair comparison needs equal capacity on both sides. After imitation, the input statistics start from the demonstrations, so the cloned policy sees inputs on the scale it was trained on.
  • Simulator state, not cameras. The course task supplies state observations; adding perception would put a second problem on top of the one we study.
The rewardexplainer

Dense, and dominated by lifting. Per decision, with \(d\) the distance from the grasp point to the object, \(\bar d\) the mean fingertip distance, and lift capped at 0.2 m:

\[ \begin{aligned} r = {} & 0.5\,(1-\tanh 10d) + 0.5\,(1-\tanh 10\bar d) + 0.1\,\text{contacts} \\ & + 0.25\,[\text{grasped}] + 50\,\text{lift}\,[\text{held}] + 5\,[\text{success}] - 5\,[\text{fell}] \end{aligned} \]

Success is a 15 cm lift with the thumb and another finger (or both jaws) on the object. After 10 decisions of success the base carries the arm along a synthetic carry route while the policy keeps the grip: +1 per decision while the object stays at least 10 cm up, −20 and the end of the episode on a drop, +10 for finishing the route.

Two weights came from failures. With a smaller lift weight the hand learned to hold the object on the table indefinitely, a reward plateau it never left. And the carry bonus is added on top of the grasp reward: when it replaced it, hovering just under the success height paid more than carrying (section 6).

Why we tuned. The untuned recipe left the gripper flat at its cloned level (~50% lift-and-carry) while its own teacher lifted ~88%: a curve 40 points under the demonstrator signals a pipeline problem, not a finding. An equal tuning budget for both effectors fixed it, and the same levels won for both: a lower learning rate (1e-4), more demonstrations (300 per object), and no interleaved cloning, which hurt both.

Confidence intervals for success ratesexplainer

Each case either succeeds or fails, so a rate over \(n\) cases is a binomial proportion. The Wilson score interval stays inside [0, 1] and behaves well near 0 and 1, where the textbook \(\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) does not:

\[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}},\qquad z = 1.96 \]

We call a difference only when the intervals separate. Pooling 5 seeds × 100 cases gives intervals of about ±4 points. Pooling treats seeds as extra cases, so we also show every seed.

9Results

RL's value grows with the end effector's complexity

For the gripper, every learned controller sits below the planner, and RL makes the cloned planner slightly worse in the full task. For the hand, RL on top of imitation adds 27 points and overtakes the hand-written grasp by 36. RL from scratch tells the other half of the story: PPO finds a gripper grasp on its own (0.61), but for the hand only 1 of 5 seeds ever finds one. The hand needs the prior to explore and RL to improve on it.

Where the hand's gain comes from. The script and its clone handle the soup can and mug but rarely the foam brick, banana or apple. RL fixes exactly those three (+41 to +65 points); on the two the script already handled it gains a little on the mug and gives some back on the can. RL is repairing the demonstrator's failures, not polishing its successes.

Lesson: training scores can mislead
During training the gripper's PPO gained about 11 points of lift-and-carry over its clone. In the full task it lost 7: its grasps failed slightly less often (21% of cases vs 28%), but the object slipped out in the maze carry far more often (12% vs 1%). Only the full task, on mazes nothing was tuned on, says what a system can do.

PPO vs RLPD

RLPD is the natural alternative for learning from demonstrations: an off-policy method that keeps the demonstrations in every training batch instead of only using them to initialise. We gave both algorithms the same start (the same cloned policy, demonstrations and 1M-step value warm-up), workers, reward and 4M-step budget, on both end effectors.

Every RLPD seed on both end effectors holds the cloned level while its actor is frozen, then collapses within 250k steps of being released; no seed gets back above 0.25 (gripper) or 0.18 (hand). Without the warm-up (RLPD's own default) the collapse simply starts at step 0. The likely mechanism is the classic one for off-policy RL from demonstrations: the critic is wrong for actions outside the data, the actor follows those errors, and in a 22- or 8-dimensional action space with contact-rich dynamics there is a lot of "outside". PPO's clipped, on-policy updates never step far from the data they were measured on. Per training hour the gap is larger still: PPO finishes 4M steps in 0.7–1.3 h, RLPD needs about 14 h.

Did the development decisions overfit?

Every cell's reserve result lands within 5 points of its development estimate, so choosing checkpoints, grips and recipes on development mazes did not tailor the systems to those mazes.

10Limits

  • Only the grasp is learned. Deliberately: it isolates what each grasp controller contributes. Carry postures are planned and differ per end effector.
  • The hand's wide-object prior comes from a side grasp that only works on the idealised rig; we adopted it on development evidence, under a rule fixed beforehand.
  • Seed spread is wide for the hand (standard objects 0.59–0.92), and two of the five hand PPO seeds in the algorithm comparison drifted late. Every seed is shown.
  • RLPD ran in a reduced form (one update per transition, two critics). A larger update ratio might change its per-step curve, at a much higher wall-clock cost.
  • Simulation only, two wide objects, and no test of the hand in PyBullet under realistic physics.
